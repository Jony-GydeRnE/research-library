Lagrangians and the Euler–Lagrange Equation

Free-particle Lagrangian in Cartesian coordinates

A free particle of mass \(m\) in two dimensions has the Lagrangian

\[ L = \frac{1}{2}\,m\!\left(\dot{x}^{2} + \dot{y}^{2}\right). \]

Switching to polar coordinates

Introduce coordinates \((r,\theta)\) with \(r > 0\) via the standard relations

\[ x = r\cos\theta, \qquad y = r\sin\theta. \]

Differentiating:

\[ \dot{x} = \dot{r}\cos\theta - r\sin\theta\;\dot{\theta}, \qquad \dot{y} = \dot{r}\sin\theta + r\cos\theta\;\dot{\theta}. \]

Squaring and summing gives

\[ \dot{x}^{2} + \dot{y}^{2} = \dot{r}^{2} + r^{2}\dot{\theta}^{2}, \]

so the Lagrangian in polar coordinates is

\[ L = \frac{1}{2}\,m\!\left(\dot{r}^{2} + r^{2}\dot{\theta}^{2}\right). \]

Euler–Lagrange equations in Cartesian coordinates

The Euler–Lagrange (EL) equation for a generalized coordinate \(q\) reads

\[ \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{q}} - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q} = 0. \]

In Cartesian coordinates, \(L\) depends on \(\dot{x}\) and \(\dot{y}\) but not on \(x\) or \(y\). The EL equations therefore give

\[ \frac{d}{dt}(m\dot{x}) = 0 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \ddot{x} = 0, \]
\[ \frac{d}{dt}(m\dot{y}) = 0 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \ddot{y} = 0. \]

These are the equations of straight-line, constant-velocity motion.

Euler–Lagrange equations in polar coordinates

The same physics in polar coordinates requires applying the EL equation to each of \(r\) and \(\theta\). First compute the needed partial derivatives:

\[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{r}} = m\dot{r}, \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{\theta}} = m r^{2}\dot{\theta}, \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial r} = m r\dot{\theta}^{2}, \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial \theta} = 0. \]

Radial equation (\(q = r\)):

\[ \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{r}} - \frac{\partial L}{\partial r} = 0 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{d}{dt}(m\dot{r}) - m r\dot{\theta}^{2} = 0. \]

Angular equation (\(q = \theta\)):

\[ \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{\theta}} - \frac{\partial L}{\partial \theta} = 0 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{d}{dt}\!\left(m r^{2}\dot{\theta}\right) = 0. \]

The angular equation states that \(m r^{2}\dot{\theta}\) is constant in time. This is conservation of angular momentum: whenever the Lagrangian has no explicit dependence on a coordinate (\(\theta\) here), the conjugate momentum \(\partial L / \partial \dot{\theta}\) is conserved. This is a concrete instance of Noether's theorem applied to rotational symmetry.

From functions to functionals: the minimization viewpoint

In ordinary calculus, if \(f\colon U \subseteq \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}\), the extrema of \(f\) satisfy \(f'(x) = 0\). The Euler–Lagrange equation is the generalization of this condition to functionals — maps from a space of functions to the reals.

A prototype functional is the length of a curve \(\gamma\colon [0,1] \to \mathbb{R}^{2}\) in Euclidean space:

\[ \mathcal{L}[\gamma] = \int_{0}^{1} \|\dot{\gamma}(t)\|\, dt. \]

More generally, an action functional takes the form \(S[\gamma] = \int_{0}^{1} L(\gamma(t),\dot{\gamma}(t))\,dt\), where \(L\) is the Lagrangian. The curves that extremize \(S\) are found by requiring \(\delta S = 0\) under all infinitesimal variations of \(\gamma\) with fixed endpoints — this is the calculus of variations, and the resulting stationarity condition is precisely the Euler–Lagrange equation.

This variational framework underpins all of classical mechanics and extends directly into field theory, where the "coordinates" \(q(t)\) are promoted to fields \(\phi(x)\) and the Lagrangian \(L\) becomes a Lagrangian density \(\mathcal{L}\). The field-theoretic Lagrangians that ultimately appear in scattering-amplitude calculations — such as the Lagrangians for colored scalar theories, the nonlinear sigma model, or Yang–Mills coupled to scalars — all derive their equations of motion from exactly this variational principle.
